The namesake

What is a tachyon?

A tachyon is a hypothetical particle that always travels faster than light. Nobody has ever detected one, and most physicists doubt they exist, but the mathematics of relativity does not rule them out, and that has made them one of physics' favourite thought experiments since the 1960s.

A fuller explainer is on its way, with interactive diagrams and simpler versions of every idea. This page is the short version.

A name for something fast

The physicist Gerald Feinberg named the particle in 1967, from the Greek tachys, “swift”. Ordinary matter can never reach the speed of light: the closer it gets, the more energy each extra bit of speed costs, without limit. A tachyon would live on the far side of that barrier. It could never slow down to the speed of light, and, strangest of all, it would go faster as it lost energy.

Arriving before it leaves

Relativity says that observers moving relative to each other can disagree about when distant events happen. For anything slower than light, they always agree on the order: a ball is thrown before it is caught. For something faster than light, some observers would see it arrive before it set off. Send messages with tachyons and you could, in principle, get a reply before you asked the question. That paradox is one of the strongest reasons physicists think tachyons cannot carry signals, or do not exist at all.

A real-world cousin: Cherenkov light

Nothing outruns light in a vacuum, but light slows down inside water or glass: in water it travels at about three quarters of its vacuum speed. Fast particles can outrun it there, and when they do they shed a cone of blue light, much as a jet outrunning sound makes a sonic boom. It is the blue glow in the water around a nuclear reactor. Physicists once searched for tachyons by looking for the same kind of light in a vacuum; none has been found.

Where the idea lives on

In modern physics, “tachyonic” usually describes not a particle but an instability: a field sitting somewhere it cannot stay, about to roll into a new state. The Higgs field is described this way, and that roll is how it gives other particles their mass.

And the editor?

Tachyon, the Markdown editor, borrows the name for its one obsession: getting there before you do. It opens in 20–35 ms and paints every keystroke inside a single frame. The animations around the screenshot on the home page are small nods to the physics above.
